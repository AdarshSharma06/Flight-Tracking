Aircraft Basics

An aircraft is a vehicle that is capable of flying through the atmosphere. Aircraft range from small single-engine airplanes to large commercial jetliners and helicopters. Understanding aircraft types, systems, and performance is fundamental to aviation.

Aircraft Types

Fixed-wing aircraft generate lift through forward motion. The wings produce an aerodynamic force that opposes gravity. Fixed-wing aircraft include:
- Single-engine piston: small aircraft powered by one piston engine (e.g., Cessna 172)
- Multi-engine piston: aircraft with two or more piston engines
- Turboprop: aircraft powered by a turbine engine driving a propeller (e.g., ATR 72)
- Jet: aircraft powered by turbofan or turbojet engines (e.g., Boeing 737, Airbus A320)
- Regional jet: smaller jet aircraft for short to medium routes (e.g., Embraer E-Jets)

Rotary-wing aircraft (helicopters) generate lift through rotating blades. Helicopters can hover, take off vertically, and land in small areas.

Aircraft Components

Fuselage: The main body of the aircraft, housing the cockpit, passengers, and cargo.

Wings: The primary lifting surfaces. Wings generate lift through their airfoil shape. The wing's angle of attack, airspeed, and wing area determine the amount of lift produced.

Empennage (tail section): Includes the horizontal stabilizer (provides pitch stability), vertical stabilizer (provides yaw stability), elevator (controls pitch), and rudder (controls yaw).

Landing gear: Supports the aircraft on the ground. Types include tricycle gear (one nose wheel, two main gear) and taildragger gear (two main gear, one tail wheel).

Powerplant: The engine and propeller or jet engine that provides thrust.

Flight Controls

Primary flight controls:
- Ailerons: Control roll (banking left or right), located on the trailing edge of the wings
- Elevator: Control pitch (nose up or down), located on the horizontal stabilizer
- Rudder: Control yaw (nose left or right), located on the vertical stabilizer

Secondary flight controls:
- Flaps: Increase lift at low speeds for takeoff and landing
- Slats: Leading-edge devices that delay stall at high angles of attack
- Spoilers: Disrupt airflow over the wing to reduce lift and increase drag
- Trim: Small surfaces that relieve control forces

Aircraft Performance

Lift is the aerodynamic force that opposes gravity. Lift depends on airspeed, air density, wing area, and the coefficient of lift. The lift equation is: L = 1/2 * rho * V^2 * S * CL

Thrust is the forward force produced by the engines. Thrust must equal drag for constant-speed flight.

Drag is the aerodynamic force that opposes motion. Types include:
- Parasite drag: caused by the aircraft's shape and surface friction
- Induced drag: caused by lift generation
- Total drag: parasite drag plus induced drag

Weight is the force of gravity acting on the aircraft. Weight includes the aircraft structure, fuel, passengers, and cargo.

Aircraft Categories by Size

Regional aircraft: 50-100 seats, short to medium range (e.g., ATR 72, Embraer E175)

Narrow-body aircraft: 100-250 seats, short to medium range (e.g., Boeing 737, Airbus A320)

Wide-body aircraft: 250-500+ seats, long range (e.g., Boeing 777, Airbus A350)

Ultra-long-range aircraft: Designed for routes exceeding 8,000 nautical miles (e.g., Boeing 787, Airbus A350-900ULR)

Aircraft Registration

Aircraft are registered with national aviation authorities and display registration marks. The prefix indicates the country of registration:
- VT- for India
- N- for United States
- G- for United Kingdom
- D- for Germany
- F- for France
