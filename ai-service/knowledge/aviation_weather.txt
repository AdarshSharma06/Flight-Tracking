Aviation Weather

Aviation weather information is critical for safe flight operations. Pilots must understand weather phenomena, interpret weather reports and forecasts, and make informed decisions about flight planning and execution.

Weather Reports and Forecasts

METAR (Meteorological Aerodrome Report): A routine weather observation issued at least once per hour. METARs contain current conditions including wind, visibility, sky condition, temperature, dew point, altimeter setting, and any significant weather phenomena.

Example METAR: DEL 031200Z 27010KT 3000 TSRA SCT030CB BKN100 32/25 Q1012

Breaking this down:
- DEL: Station identifier (Delhi)
- 031200Z: Date and time (3rd at 1200Z)
- 27010KT: Wind from 270 degrees at 10 knots
- 3000: Visibility 3000 meters
- TSRA: Thunderstorm with rain
- SCT030CB: Scattered clouds at 3000 feet with cumulonimbus
- BKN100: Broken clouds at 10,000 feet
- 32/25: Temperature 32°C, dew point 25°C
- Q1012: Altimeter 1012 hPa

TAF (Terminal Aerodrome Forecast): A weather forecast for a specific airport, issued every 6 hours for major airports. TAFs cover a 24 to 30 hour period.

SIGMET (Significant Meteorological Information): Weather advisories for significant weather phenomena affecting a wide area. SIGMETs cover phenomena such as severe turbulence, severe icing, thunderstorms, volcanic ash, and tropical cyclones.

AIRMET (Airmen's Meteorological Information): Weather advisories for phenomena that may be hazardous to light aircraft, such as moderate turbulence, moderate icing, and mountain obscuration.

Weather Phenomena

Wind: Wind direction and speed are reported in knots. Wind direction is the direction from which the wind blows, measured clockwise from true north. A wind of 27010KT means wind from 270 degrees (west) at 10 knots.

Gusts: Wind gusts are sudden increases in wind speed. A gust factor of 10 knots or more indicates gusty conditions.

Wind shear: A sudden change in wind speed or direction over a short distance. Wind shear is particularly dangerous during takeoff and landing. Low-level wind shear occurs below 2,000 feet AGL.

Thunderstorms: Thunderstorms contain severe turbulence, icing, hail, and microbursts. Cumulonimbus (CB) clouds indicate thunderstorm activity. Pilots should avoid thunderstorms by at least 20 nautical miles.

Turbulence: Irregular air motion that causes aircraft to bounce or vibrate. Categories include:
- Light: Slight, erratic changes in altitude or attitude
- Moderate: Changes in altitude or attitude, but aircraft remains in control
- Severe: Large, sudden changes in altitude or attitude, aircraft may be momentarily out of control
- Extreme: Aircraft violently tossed, practically impossible to control

Icing: Ice accumulation on aircraft surfaces. Structural icing occurs when supercooled water droplets freeze on contact with the aircraft. Icing degrades aerodynamic performance and can be dangerous.

Visibility: The greatest distance at which objects can be seen and identified with the naked eye. Reduced visibility affects approach and landing minima.

Ceiling: The height above ground level of the lowest layer of clouds reported as broken or overcast. A broken layer means 5/8 to 7/8 of the sky is covered. An overcast layer means 8/8 of the sky is covered.

Weather Decision Making

Pilots must evaluate weather conditions before and during flight. Key considerations include:
- Current and forecast conditions at departure, destination, and alternate airports
- En-route weather along the planned flight path
- Aircraft performance limitations in various weather conditions
- Personal minimums and experience level
- Availability of alternates and escape routes
